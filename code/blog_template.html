Between 1788 and 1868 Britain shipped about {{stated.conv_162|int}} convicts to the other side of the world. Wikipedia's volunteers have listed their ships colony by colony. I mapped all {{source.n_main|int}} voyages, and then followed the trail on to French Guiana, Sakhalin and the Andaman Islands.

On 13 May 1787 eleven ships left Portsmouth carrying more than {{stated.ff_over|int}} people, over half of them convicts. They reached Botany Bay {{stated.ff_days|int}} days later, in January 1788, and moved on to Sydney Cove, where they met the Eora people. That was the start of eighty years in which Britain used the Australian colonies as its prison. Men, women and children sentenced to "transportation beyond the seas" were put on hired merchant ships and sent to New South Wales, Van Diemen's Land (now Tasmania), Norfolk Island and, last of all, Western Australia.

English Wikipedia has a list of the convict ships for each of those colonies. They are patchy, and for most voyages they give only a ship's name, the port it left and the date it arrived, but between them they hold {{source.rows_total|int}} rows. Leave out the transfers between Australian ports, a few voyages listed twice and the ships that carried boys from Parkhurst prison, and {{source.n_main|int}} voyages from overseas remain. Wikipedia's article on convicts in Australia says "about {{stated.conv_164|int}} convicts were transported to the Australian colonies between 1788 and 1868 onboard {{stated.ships_806|int}} ships", so the lists hold {{cover.n_main_pct_806|int}}% of the ships.

The flood after Waterloo

The first years were slow: {{peak.n_1790s|int}} voyages in the 1790s and {{peak.n_1800s|int}} in the 1800s, while Britain was fighting France. Then the war ended, and the ships came. Between 1815 and 1840 the lists hold {{peak.n_1815_1840|int}} arrivals, {{peak.pct_1815_1840|int}}% of the whole. The busiest year was {{peak.year|raw}}, with {{peak.n|int}}, and the 1830s saw {{peak.n_1830s|int}}, more than one a fortnight.

Then the traffic moved. New South Wales stopped taking convicts in 1840, and from 1841 to 1849 the lists hold {{peak.vdl_4149|int}} arrivals in Van Diemen's Land, {{peak.nfk_4149|int}} at Norfolk Island and just {{peak.nsw_4149|int}} in Sydney. Transportation to Van Diemen's Land ended in 1853. Western Australia, which asked for convicts to build its colony, took {{col.wa.n|int}} shiploads between {{col.wa.first|raw}} and {{col.wa.last|raw}}. The last of them, Hougoumont, reached Fremantle on 9 January 1868.

Cork, London and Portsmouth

Most ships left from the Thames or the Solent. But the single busiest port in the lists is Cork, with {{irish.cork|int}} voyages, ahead of Portsmouth and London; Dublin had {{irish.dublin|int}}. Irish ports account for {{irish.n|int}} of the {{irish.n_ei|int}} voyages from Britain and Ireland, {{irish.pct|int}}%, and almost all of them went to Sydney: {{irish.nsw_pct|int}}% of New South Wales's ships from the British Isles were Irish. Another {{indian.n|int}} voyages sailed from India, Ceylon, Mauritius or Singapore. Where the tables give numbers these carried only a handful of convicts each, some of them what the notes call "military convicts".

From 252 days to 89

The lists don't say when ships left, but the tables of the first three fleets and the articles on some individual ships do. For {{days.n|int}} voyages I have both dates. The first three fleets took a median of {{days.eras.0.median|int}} days, and Lady Juliana, the Second Fleet's women's ship, took {{days.slow.days|int}}. By the 1820s and 1830s the median was {{days.eras.2.median|int}} days. The fastest in the sample was Scindian, the first convict ship to Western Australia, in {{days.fast.days|int}} days in {{days.fast.y|raw}}.

The Second Fleet

The worst voyages were among the first. The Second Fleet's contractors were paid a flat fee for every convict they took on board, "whether they landed alive or not". Of the {{stated.sf_embarked|int}} convicts embarked, {{stated.sf_died|int}} died during the voyage, {{deaths.sf_pct|int}}%, and {{stated.sf_sick|int}} landed sick. On the Third Fleet a year later, {{deaths.tf_deaths|int}} of {{deaths.tf_boarded|int}} died at sea, {{deaths.tf_pct|f1}}%. On the {{deaths.art_n|int}} later voyages whose ship articles give the numbers, {{deaths.art_deaths|int}} of {{deaths.art_embarked|int}} convicts died, {{deaths.art_pct|f1}}%. Shipwreck remained a danger: when Amphitrite went down at the start of her voyage, {{stated.amph_women|int}} women convicts and {{stated.amph_children|int}} children drowned.

One in seven

About {{stated.women|int}} of the convicts, one in seven, were women, and some {{stated.women_theft_pct|int}}% of them had been convicted of theft, usually petty. The lists rarely say who a ship carried, so I can only identify {{women.flagged|int}} women's voyages from them. A table in Wikipedia's article on convict women shows how the balance changed in New South Wales: women were {{women.pop_first_pct|int}}% of the convicts in 1788 and {{women.pop_last_pct|int}}% in {{women.pop_last_year|raw}}. Western Australia took no women at all.

Somebody's land

None of these ships landed on empty country. Wikipedia's article on penal transportation puts it plainly: violent conflict on the Australian frontier between Indigenous Australians and the colonists "began only months after the First Fleet landed, lasting over a century". Convicts were on both sides of it. Some were killed on the frontier, some took part in killings such as the Myall Creek massacre, and in the Hawkesbury and Nepean Wars a group of Irish convicts joined the Eora, Gandangara, Dharug and Tharawal nations fighting the colonists.

Not only Britain

Australia was Britain's second destination. Before the American Revolution, convicts went to Virginia and Maryland, "commonly accepted" to be {{stated.am_1717_low|int}} people, with estimates up to {{stated.am_keneally|int}}. France shipped more than {{stated.guiana|int}} to the Devil's Island system in French Guiana between 1852 and 1953, and about {{stated.newcal|int}} to New Caledonia. Russia sent convicts to katorga in Siberia and set up a penal colony on Sakhalin in 1857. British India sent prisoners of the 1857 rebellion to the Andaman Islands, where the Cellular Jail later held independence activists; the penal settlement closed in 1947.

How I did this

Everything comes from English Wikipedia, saved in 2026 with revision numbers: the four colony lists of convict ships, the tables in the First, Second and Third Fleet articles, {{source.n_ship_articles|int}} convict ship articles, and the articles on convicts in Australia, penal transportation and the other penal colonies. I count a voyage from overseas once, by its year of arrival. Convict numbers, departure dates and deaths come from the Western Australia and Norfolk Island tables, the fleet tables and sentences in ship articles, kept only where the article's arrival date matches the list. The lists give numbers of convicts for only {{cover.n_conv|int}} voyages, so I don't add them up to a national total; Wikipedia's own figure is about {{stated.conv_162|int}}. The routes on the map are stylised sailing tracks, not logged ones. The paper sets out every decision.
